    Aptamer-SPION Magnetic Filtration for Malaria-Infected Blood

    iGEM McMaster x SHAD at Mac Case CompetitionMar 2026Team of 5

    Published as a conference abstract in the URNCST Journal · doi:10.26685/urncst.1125

    What if a magnet could pull malaria out of donated blood? Our filtration concept could let more Canadians become blood donors.

    Read more

    The problem: Canadians returning from malaria-endemic regions must wait three months before donating blood, which adds to Canada's blood shortage.

    Our solution

    1. Donor blood is incubated with aptamers bound to superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs). The aptamers bind PfEMP1, a malaria marker on the surface of infected red blood cells.
    2. A gate valve controls the flow rate into two primary high-gradient magnetic separation zones that capture the tagged cells, and a secondary zone removes remaining debris while uninfected blood drains through by gravity.
    3. The step fits into the existing donation pipeline after nucleic acid screening and before standard processing and storage.
    4. Removing infected cells this way could shorten the deferral period and expand donor eligibility.

    What we did

    1. Research. Evaluated diagnostic feasibility, biological implementation, cost per blood unit, and safety requirements such as complete removal of SPIONs before transfusion.
    2. Prototype. 3D modelled and printed a working model of the filtration pathway to show how the system functions.
    3. Pitch. Presented our research to judges in Phase 2 of the competition and placed second. This work became my first publication, a conference abstract in the URNCST Journal.

    PathFinder: Hazard-Detecting Goggles for Snowboarders with Low Vision

    Design Project 4McMaster iBioMed, Level ITeam Manager · Coding SubteamTeam of 4

    Snowboarding goggles that scan the slope ahead and buzz faster as obstacles get closer, built for a 67-year-old client with limited peripheral vision.

    Watch the prototype video I edited
    Read more

    Objective: Identify a real need for a member of the local community and develop a solution based on a careful assessment of the client's needs.

    The client

    • 67-year-old man with multiple sclerosis
    • Wants to snowboard
    • Limited peripheral vision
    • Cost concerns

    How it works

    1. A servo motor sweeps a time-of-flight distance sensor, housed discreetly on top of the goggles, across a 120° field ahead.
    2. Two vibration motors on the headband buzz faster as a detected object gets closer.
    3. A single red LED warns when snow is blocking the sensor, and a push button controls the device.

    What I did

    1. Led the team. As team manager, I set weekly agendas, booked design studio time, ran TA meetings, kept everyone on schedule, and made the final call when ideas competed.
    2. Pivoted on client feedback. We first planned a seated snowboard with sensors. Talking to the client showed his real concerns were reaction time and vision, not balance, so we redesigned around hazard-detecting goggles.
    3. Refined the design. After design review, we replaced proximity LEDs with vibration cues, since a visual signal doesn't help a user with poor vision. We also moved the motors from the temples to the headband after learning that vibration near the temples can cause headaches and eye strain.
    4. Code and electronics. I co-wrote the code from a shared flowchart and pseudocode. I wired the distance sensor, servo, push button, LED and both vibration motors, and soldered the push-button connections, which was my first time soldering.
    5. Debugged under deadline. I built troubleshooting time into the schedule, expecting integration problems. When the housed sensor wouldn't rotate, we traced the cause to taped wires acting like a spring against the servo. On presentation day I set a backup plan and asked the design studio staff for advice. Their suggested servo change got the prototype working fully.
    6. Presented. I presented how the design works and the conclusion, and shot and edited our prototype video. We were selected as one of the top 4 teams to present at the iBioMed Final Showcase.

    Spine Align: Squat Form-Correction Device

    Design Project 3McMaster iBioMed, Level ITeam Coordinator · Coding SubteamTeam of 4

    A wearable that senses your back angle mid-squat, warns you with a vibration, then physically pushes your spine back into position.

    Watch the prototype video I edited
    Read more

    Objective: Design a device that improves accessibility by automating a common task, taking input from a sensor and giving output to the user. Our users were beginner gym-goers learning to squat, since improper form is behind the most common gym injury, to the back.

    How it works

    1. An orientation sensor on the user's back streams pitch readings, which the code smooths with a rolling average.
    2. If the back angle deviates past 45°, a vibration motor warns the user.
    3. DC motors then drive a gear train and rack and pinion that press on the upper or lower back to correct the angle.
    4. Once form is corrected, the rack retracts and the motors stop.

    What I did

    1. Concept. Brainstormed and sketched concepts, then used a Pugh matrix to select the final design. After design review, we replaced a single rotating arm with two fixed arms, one on the upper back and one on the lower back, for faster, more accurate correction.
    2. Code. On the coding subteam, I planned the program with a flowchart and pseudocode, wrote my share of the functions, and integrated them into one program.
    3. Hardware. Wired the orientation sensor, two DC motors and the vibration motor on a breadboard, then tested and tuned the code against live sensor data.
    4. Fixing the fit under pressure. In the week of the design expo, the printed housing turned out too small for the electronics because of a measurement error. I proposed printing an extra piece to give the DC motors room, cut openings in the housing, and braced the motors so they couldn't shift. The gear train then ran smoothly for the demo.
    5. Delivery. Demonstrated the device at the design expo and answered technical questions. I also kept detailed meeting minutes as team coordinator, co-wrote an industry-style memo and design history file, and shot and edited the prototype video.

    Lesson learned: Verify the CAD measurements against the real components before printing, and reprint rather than patch when the schedule allows.

    SynuSense: Nasal-Delivered PET Tracer for Multiple System Atrophy

    McMaster iBioMedTeam of 5Investor pitch

    MSA is often misdiagnosed as Parkinson's until autopsy. SynuSense pairs a PET tracer with a nasal spray to give clinicians a definitive answer.

    Read more

    The problem: Multiple system atrophy (MSA-P) causes movement, balance and autonomic dysfunction, but no current test gives a definite diagnosis. Between 12% and 29% of autopsy-proven MSA cases were diagnosed as Parkinson's disease during the patient's life.

    Our concept

    1. [18F] ACI-12589 is a PET tracer that binds specifically to the misfolded alpha-synuclein aggregates that drive MSA.
    2. We proposed loading the tracer into a hydrogel delivered as a nasal spray, so it can travel along the olfactory and trigeminal nerves directly into the brain.
    3. A PET scan then shows the distinct MSA pattern of aggregation in glial cells, separating it from Parkinson's and Lewy body dementia.

    The pitch

    • ~25,000 undiagnosed Americans targeted
    • ~CA$3,240 median cost per scan
    • $10M investment ask
    • 17-year path to market

    We built the full case: disease mechanism, the diagnostic gap, competitor analysis, a cost breakdown, a funding allocation, and a regulatory roadmap from patents to post-market surveillance.

    Targeted Nanoparticle Therapy for Atherosclerosis

    McMaster iBioMedTeam of 5Research proposal

    Calming artery inflammation at the source by sending a drug only where plaque is forming.

    Read more

    The problem: Atherosclerosis is the leading cause of cardiovascular disease worldwide. The NF-κB pathway drives the inflammation behind plaque growth, but the drug that blocks it (PS-341, a proteasome inhibitor) would harm healthy cells if given systemically.

    Our proposal

    1. Nanoparticles are coated with antibodies against ICAM-1, VCAM-1 and LOX-1, markers found on inflamed, plaque-prone artery walls.
    2. The nanoparticles carry PS-341 directly to those regions, blocking the proteasome from releasing NF-κB and interrupting the inflammation feedback loop.
    3. Evidence from the literature suggests this also helps macrophages export cholesterol to HDL for removal by the liver.

    We mapped the NF-κB pathway step by step, reviewed experimental evidence for targeting and drug efficacy, and defined patients with advanced atherosclerosis as the target group.

    Bleaching Bad: Engineered Antioxidant Circuit to Protect Coral

    IBEHS 2PB2Apr 2026Team of 5

    A genetic circuit in marine bacteria that only switches on when the reef is both too hot and under oxidative stress, then releases antioxidants to keep coral and algae together.

    Explore the reef monitoring dashboard
    Read more

    The problem: Coral reefs support about 25% of all marine species. Heat stress makes their symbiotic algae produce reactive oxygen species (ROS), which damage the partnership until the algae are expelled and the coral bleaches. Current strategies focus on environmental management and don't respond quickly to extreme heat events.

    How the circuit works

    1. The circuit lives in Vibrio natriegens, a fast-growing marine bacterium suited to salty reef environments.
    2. Two sensors act as an AND gate: an OxyR-responsive promoter detects ROS, and a temperature-sensitive promoter activates above 25 °C.
    3. Only when both are triggered is the cI repressor fully shut off, releasing the pR promoter to produce the antioxidant enzymes superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase.
    4. An SsrA degradation tag on TetR stops the system from firing during short, harmless ROS bursts.

    What we did

    1. Designed the construct. Mapped the circuit as an SBOL diagram and assembled all 23 parts into a 13,685 bp plasmid in Benchling using Golden Gate assembly, with primers for PCR amplification.
    2. Planned wet-lab validation. Designed a restriction digest to verify assembly, plus GFP reporter, Western blot and spectrophotometry experiments to test each part of the logic.
    3. Modelled it. Built a dynamic SimBiology model of every reaction. Under sustained heat and ROS the circuit produced large amounts of antioxidants, though the AND gate proved less sensitive to its inputs than expected.
    4. Built a dashboard. Made an interactive website that maps reef locations and plots temperature against ROS over time.
